Seas of Green and Brown

For Brass Ensemble

When Aaron Reising and I were living in Estonia, we spent a lot of time talking about the place we had just left: our homes in Ohio. It brought up a lot of thoughts about the corner of Ohio I spent my childhood, down right at the base of the foothills which eventually lead into Appalachia proper. While much of my culture is midwestern- corn and chili- I was fortunate enough to be taught what a dulcimer is, Appalachian folk songs, and I was brought down to the Hocking Hills region practically daily. It is that corner of Ohio- the part where the old forests start- that inspire this piece.

The title references the greens and browns seen in these old forests. The colors muted by morning fog. For a brass ensemble of any skill level, this piece presents a challenge. For young ensembles its the challenge of endurance, for advanced ensembles it's seeing into the music and bringing out details in hidden melodies smeared by misaligned rhythms. Either way, a texture is created where brass tones blend to form the same hazy fog that hangs over the forests I missed so much when I found myself waking up to ice and snow.

I want to thank all the musicians who brought this piece to life for the first time- for a group of undergraduate brass majors with no time to rehearse it still remains an achievement that I am extremely proud of.

If you would like to perform this piece, please email me at mwrobertson02@gmail.com and I will send you the parts :) The score can be downloaded from the embedded pdf on this page.

Program Note

In the Spring of 2024, while living in Tallinn, Estonia, I spent (for the first time in about 6 years) a significant amount of time away from playing the tuba. While I still picked up the horn a couple of times each week, I did not spend enough time with it. This was in comparison to the previous semesters of up to 40 hours of rehearsal and practice each week, making for quite a contrast. Among many other things, I missed playing, and I missed the sound of brass instruments. Looking ahead to working with marching bands and restarting my regular playing routine, I spent time dwelling on the characteristics of brass music. This piece, Seas of Green and Brown, is a meditation on those feelings of missing something. What better medium for the meditation than the sounds that I was missing so dearly?

The title comes from a part of those emotions– that there are seemingly insignificant or blasé details that remain so vivid amongst memories that amplify the sensation of missing something. The forests of southern Ohio, particularly within the Appalachian mountains, arehard to forget anyway, but their colors remained so vivid within my memory. Being 4,500miles made me linger on their detail. While this music is time spent thinking about so manythings, it can only have one title. In that same way, despite the much more significant memories of friends, family, making music, and the culture that I call home, it was the colorsof the Ohioan forests that stood at the forefront. All those memories could only have one detail on their "front cover".

While not the original compositional goal, the depth in this music is developed from the detail within the dynamics of each phrase that make up the thicker textures. There is hardly a single beat without some sort of dynamic gesture written into it, and this complexity differing between each player makes for not just a thick texture, but a fluid one (along with a lengthy engraving process…). The piece is constructed in three sections, beginning with shorter phrases that grow and thicken into a moving texture where melodies rise and decayagain. This texture then intensifies into a much more turbulent and detailed phrase which resolves into a resounding and powerful harmony where the brass soundbecomes more pronounced. This sound then decays and comes back to rest; the phrasesshorten again, finishing with one final push back into silence.
