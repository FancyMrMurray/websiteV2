Sitting And Listening

October 13, 2026

So I know that I probably do too much to like. Focus on any one thing. And yeah I want to be coding and making cool interactive art all day long. But I do have this side quest at the Experimental Sound Studio as their Archives Engineer (a title I made up but was validated by a real archivist and engineer in New York) transferring analog reel-to-reel tapes to digital WAV files and building their database and storage servers (with a little bit of network config mixed in).

The whole point in me writing this is that I finally hit my first big benchmark in this position. Was it standardizing all the data formats in our archival indexes? Writing scripts to comb through thousands of hand copied labels all in different formats? Spending two months building a test database server using an old donated PC from like 2014? No. I finally finished transferring a whole collection of reel-to-reels. Yeah. That one takes the blue ribbon for “hardest task” over the last *checks watch* 6 MONTHS.

For anybody who isn’t familiar with analog audio, or just like older media formats in general, the brutal slog of reel-to-reel tapes is the fact that they have to be played all the way through at the original speed. It’s not like a CD where a song can be ripped by just reading all the information off the disc really fast. If a tape has 30 minutes of audio content on it, we have to put it on the machine and let it play for 30 minutes. You can see how 102 tapes that are all between 30 and 90 minutes (and half of which have two sides, also 30 to 90 minutes) can become a never ending Herculean monumental assignment.

But there’s this really cool thing that happens when you sit there and listen to these things. I feel like I know the performers. I can’t talk about the collection itself necessarily yet (hopefully I’ll update with a link once ESS actually releases it) but it belongs to one particular musician. There’s a lot of stuff- rehearsals, concerts, workshops, public forums, social events. Mf would just throw up the microphones for anything. It paints a really clear picture actually, of what he and his musicians felt was valuable enough to buy tape and bother setting up the equipment for. These were the days before portable recorders- even cassette tapes weren’t a thing yet.

I know it probably isn’t as flashy as the other stuff I do, but yesterday as I pulled the last tape off of the machine and realized there wasn’t another one to put on… I got kind of emotional. It was the first time in a while that I had done something where there wasn’t a more efficient workaround. There wasn’t some shortcut to help me get to the end. People always ask when we show them, “there has to be an easier way.” Nope. For the non-profits, the small folks, the people who lack institutional infrastructure, there will never be a better way than just throwing these bad boys on the tape deck and hitting play. And lemme tell you- it is a worthwhile endeavor.

Please stay tuned for some cool projects coming up- I am starting to work on a larger collection of web based audio pieces and I have a new MIDI controller sitting half-soldered on my desk. I get to take a step back from archival work for the moment to focus on some more artistic things and I’m so happy that I get to do that on a good note. Thanks!! (one audio clip as a treat…)
