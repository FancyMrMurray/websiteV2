Semantic Sound and Subtractive Synthesis

Field Recording as a Powerful Creative Tool in Contemporary Music

November 12, 2025

This paper was written and published as a part of the November 2025 "Principles of Music Composing" conferenced hosted by the Lithuanian Composers' Union. If you would like to reference the "official" version of this paper, please look for the LCU journal's 2025 edition.

I would be remiss not to to thank Dr. Dina Lentsner at Capital University for her mentorship on this research.

Abstract

In the past century, technological advances have consistently expanded access to personal recording devices, signal processing, and other sound production tools. Using Michel Chion (1994) and Aaron Einbond’s (2013) framework of the creative process as subtractive synthesis, I aim to establish a model for which describes the processes involved in listening to field recorded sounds in the context of electroacoustic music.

Chion’s “semantic mode” presents the opportunity to view sonic objects as cues which can be decoded by a listener given they have the appropriate semantic knowledge (Antony, 1997). This process of recognizing sound can then in turn affect the independent process of interpreting the sound cues and their associated semantic meanings. I analyse possible interpretations by observing various semantic parameters which describe the sounds’ meanings and potential for various interpretations by listeners. The three parameters I use for analysis are defined by using existing scholarship: Chion (1994) describes linguistic semantics, Feld (1996) discusses the semantics of place, and Gallagher (2014) describes semantics of spatialization. From these independent semantic parameters this article develops a broader approach which considers the intersection of multiple types of semantic information.

To demonstrate my model, I offer a brief analysis of three pieces using field recordings as main components of their compositions: For Ruth by Annea Lockwood (2023), So Much More by Annika Socolofsky (2021), and Places of Sounds and Words by Henrik Hellstenius (2014). I establish that sources for field recordings serve as what Einbond describes as “totality” from which smaller pieces can be filtered. This subtractive relationship allows a listener to follow the connection in reverse via recognition, providing semantic information which nuances audience interpretation of music.

Introduction

“Negotiating the ’out there’ with the ’in here’” is how John Levack Drever (2017) describes the practice of field recording. The profoundness of that phrase should not be lost in the world where recording sound in the field has become an elementary task: open up a mobile phone, run the audio recorder, and hit start. The world of recordists, artists, and history Drever describes has had its borders opened to everyone who has ever recorded something happening in the world.

Peering backwards in time to Pierre Schaeffer and his Etudes de Bruits and Ludwig Koch’s instructional guide on fox hunting (which features a six-minute spliced together “composition” of a full hunt), field recording is a well established method which is both a medium and technique for composition. This article focuses on the use of field recording in contemporary classical composition, moreso the use of recorded sound, not the practice of recording sound.

The purpose of this paper is to establish a model that describes how field recordings create a connection between a listener and the original recorded sound. The components of this model are the concepts of semantic sound, semantic knowledge, and Aaron Einbond’s (2013) theory of subtractive synthesis. First, I establish that field recordings, defined broadly as any recorded sound whose source may be recognized by a listener, carry within them “semantic sound, i.e., sound which has a meaning to be decoded (Fagagnini, 2023). A listener can then recognize and decode that semantic information by exercising the causal and semantic listening modes (Chion, 1994), and then interpret the sound’s meaning in a work of music.

Listening for Semantic Sound

At the fundamental level, examining the function of field recording in music requires acknowledgement of the listener. As such, I center the construction of this model on the listener, and the act of listening. To do this, I use Michel Chion’s (1994) conception of listening articulated through the “three modes of listening.” The first, causal listening, sees a listener attempt to identify the cause of a sound: its source. The second, semantic listening, involves the decoding of meaning from sounds, like a grandfather clock’s chimes translating to numerical time. The third mode, “reduced,” is comparable to Pierre Schaeffer’s (1966) term “acousmatic” in which sound is disconnected from any source or meaning and is focused on the characteristics of the sound in a more abstract sense.

Acousmatic listening and the freedom of artistic interpretation that comes with it is a separate discussion that is not missing in broader musical scholarship. This study, however, stems from the first two types of listening that contrast the third, and thus, using Schaeffer’s term, I propose to refer to them as non-acousmatic. The model explored in this article focuses on the point in the process of listening when listeners are in the state of identifying and connecting sounds to their own experience; hereafter referred to as recognition.

Within non-acousmatic listening resides both the causal and semantic listening modes, which requires a clear distinction. Causal listening is the mode in which someone is “listening to a sound in order to gain information about its source” (Chion 1994, 25). Then there is semantic listening, which entails the listening for languages and other codes, sounds that can carry meaning (Chion 1994, 28).

This sort of sound, which carries a meaning, can be labeled “semantic sound” (Fagagnini, 2023) and it is used consistently by sound designers, especially in interactive media like video games, to give feedback to a player. Additionally, they have common functions seen in spoken language and audible codes (like Morse). Causal listening and semantic listening are independent from one another, and the synthesis of the two creates the ability for a listener to fully recognise sounds, their sources, and their meanings.

This act of recognition can be alternately described using terms from scholarship on semantics and meaning-theory. Whereas through Chion’s listening there is a relationship between sound source and decoded meaning, Louise Antony (1997) describes the relationships between something having meaning, and knowing that meaning. The relation is simple: anything can have meaning, but that meaning requires someone to have knowledge of it in order to decode it. This is why words are different between dialects of a language— everyone possesses different knowledge of what that word means. These two perspectives place the process of recognition between considering sound’s source, its meaning, and the knowledge required to decode the meaning.

Further, Antony summarizes various meaning-theories by writing that, “semantics has nothing to do with psychology” (177). Here, in music, psychology is the content of thought, which I consider as the interpretive aspect of listening. Chion (1994) provides a clear- cut analytical method of listening, and while meaning-theory recognises this style of recognition, its elaboration to include interpretation is necessary to consider for the purpose of art. Combining these approaches into one function creates a form of listening which involves firstly recognition via knowledge of meaning, and secondly interpretation of that meaning.

Sources and Resulting Sound: the Model

Considering that recognition and interpretation are independent, and yet a part of the same process, I arrive at the conclusion that a sound’s meaning to a listener must at least carry the potential to affect interpretation. Gallagher (2015) also refers to the “affectiveness” of field recording in his analysis of the medium, although his writing focuses on the concept of “worldly vibration” (Grosz, 2008). Where in his writing the “affectiveness” is supported by referencing Grosz, I aim to support it with Einbond’s theory of subtractive synthesis (2013). The question here is: where is the semantic information sourced from, what is it representative of, and how are those two answers linked?

According to Einbond, field recording is subtractive: it is a process of filtering down a source into a smaller piece. Einbond’s theory of subtractive synthesis is based on the conventional methods of sound synthesis where filters (either analog circuits or digital algorithms) remove frequency components from sounds in order to manipulate their timbre. This was established in contrast to additive synthesis– combining frequencies to create new sounds. Einbond states that on a metaphorical level subtractive synthesis’ principle of removal can be applied broadly in any case where there is what he refers to as an “acoustic totality.” Following Einbond, I consider field recording to be subtractive: it utilizes the recording device, microphones, the start/stop buttons, and any processing/splicing done in post production where conventional filters would be used. These new forms of filtering form a creative process through which the resulting sound is still inherent and indicative of its original source.

Einbond considers all recorded sound to be to some degree subtractive. Microphones all remove certain frequencies; storing sound either in analog or digital formats can never be perfect; and reproducing those sounds over loudspeakers is not equivalent to hearing the sounds originally. While this means that all recorded sound is subtractive in nature, my conclusion for this paper’s purpose is that the core of Einbond’s ideas are about the creative process first and foremost. The intentionality behind the process which artists use gives the label of subtractive its meaning. Rennie (2017) writes that a recordist’s presence is inherent to the content of a field recording; that it resides in the “shadows.” It is these “shadows” which give depth to the creative process at the core of Einbond’s (2013) idea of subtractive synthesis.

I conceptualize the complete model of subtractive synthesis by combining Einbond’s theory with Chion’s (1994) semantic listening and Antony’s (1997) separation of meaning and knowledge of meaning, and identify the following stages of the process:

  • First, existing sound sources are recorded.
  • Second, the processing of the audio occurs, including distortive processes which may inhibit the ability to recognize the original sound.
  • Third, artists implement that sound into a musical composition.
  • Fourth, a listener interacts with the sound by listening and possibly recognizing it, for which semantic listening and knowledge of meaning are conditions.
  • Only after all that takes place may a listener use their own semantic knowledge to interpret the sound, thus drawing a connection from listener back to sound source, given the conditions of recognition.

This last component, where a listener’s personal experience shapes interpretation, is where the significance of field recording’s potential lies. It is a listener’s own memories and experiences which shape the ways they may feel about certain sounds. The musical composition itself plays a large role in this, too, but it does not necessarily determine a piece’s semantics as intimated by a particular sound. Also, I argue that recognition does not require a placement of a sound’s source (i.e. “I know that sound came from a car” or similar statement). Most importantly, object recognition is possible on a subconscious level (Karkowski 2018, 174), which implies this whole process does not necessarily have to take place actively. Listeners can be recognizing sounds without thinking about it— a process which still affects the interpretation.

Specifying Semantic Parameters

In the context of sound, semantic information is abstract at best. Relating it back to semiotics, sound’s capacity to be a sign is extremely nuanced and wide-ranging. My goal is not to establish clear-cut categories for “types of information,” “styles of sound object,” or any other possible delineation. Rather, I am to answer the question “what can exist inside a field recording?” To be clear: the following parameters by which semantic information can appear and be analysed are non-comprehensive, not exclusive of one another, and exist at varying levels of detail and saturation. Each parameter is a point of intersection where some type of meaning emerges and can be isolated for analysis.

Language is one of the parameters by which sound carries information. Of course, spoken language relies on sound as its medium and as such, can be recorded. This style of semantic information is supported by Chion (1994) as well as film studies. Chion writes: “I call semantic listening that which refers to a code or a language to interpret a message: spoken language, of course, as well as Morse and other such codes” (28).

What exactly are these “codes” which Chion refers to? His mention of Morse code alludes to the inclusion of languages of all cryptographic and technological nature. Then, it seems plausible to include the audible sounds of dial-up internet or digital cable in this category as a language for computers. Furthermore, linguistic semantics includes all aspects of spoken word and audible codes, including tone of voice, accent, cadence, among other elements. The countless means by which humans communicate using their bodies may be organized in two categories/parameters - place and space.

Place is a parameter dealing with cultural regions and the sonic artifacts that point to particular regions, extensively explored in the writings of anthropologist Steven Feld (1996, 2015). The scholar coined the term “acoustemology,” which “engages acoustics at the plane of the audible— akoustos— to inquire into sounding as simultaneously social and material” (2015, 12). It is the word “social” which prompts the label “place” not to mean a physical location, but rather the people, society, communities, and natural world which we would refer to as cultural. Because cultures tend to occupy specific geographic places, I choose to label this parameter as “place” - the concept that also allows for abstract places, such as the internet and other digital space within it, as well as anything within Benedict Anderson’s description of imagined communities (1983).

The sounds that communicate place are the sounds unique to particular regions, including natural sounds which imply their geolocation, city sounds which imply lifestyle and community structures, occupational sounds, accents, architectural sounds, or words and phrases unique to a particular culture. Notice how more than one of these specific objects may also be interpreted via the parameter of language. Parameters are not exclusive of one another, and I strongly believe that if they were to somehow be graphed visually, they would overlap more often than not. It is these overlaps which allows for some clarity, too: perhaps “place” can be identified by examining the elements of a voice that cannot be analysed by “language.”

In this same line of logic, space is the parameter pertaining to physical locations that includes semantic cues which are not specific enough to indicate an exact region. This is much more scientific in nature than Feld’s concept of knowing through sound — in fact, Feld specifies that his own ideas are not scientific by citing an acoustics textbook: “Answers to such questions do not necessarily engage acoustics on the formal scientific plane that investigates the physical components of sound’s materiality (Kinsler et al.: 1999)” (Feld: 2015).

To define space, I first acknowledge the soundscape artists comprising the World Soundscape Project, namely R Murray Schafer, Barry Truax, and Hildegard Westerkamp, whose investigations of physical spaces of sound are fundamental to sound studies. However, Michael Gallagher (2015) writes clearly about the science of the reproduction of these spaces, specifically, how field recordings and the associated technology actually conveys space. Keeping with the scientific delineation between space and place, I use Gallagher as the primary support here: “I suggest that field recordings can be understood as contributing to the production of space. Field recordings generate three-dimensional vibrational spaces—dynamic, fluid, and temporary but spaces nonetheless” (561). Gallagher’s definition of space maintains the “affective” quality as well, keeping space a parameter which does not limit a listener’ interpretation.

Because space and place are so closely related, they often overlap, however, their compositional use is still distinct. Space is not just a semantic, but also a sonic parameter that can be generated by reverb devices and manipulated by a composer in minute detail. In electroacoustic music, the acoustic concept of space is synonymous with reverb; as a semantic parameter, it does not differ except to include the communication to a listener the characteristics of the physical environment. Place, however, is not something that can be loaded into a plug-in and automated inside a DAW. I often think of a cathedral’s reverb — the reverberations and the “washiness” of the sound are communicating space, while the implications of being inside a religious space are communicating place.

Applying these, or any other, parameters to a sound object requires recognizing that the purpose of analyses is to evaluate the potential of a sound to be a semantic sign. The model for recognition and further interpretation by the listener exists for the purpose of defining the manner in which a field recording or recorded sound object connect the listener with its source and any deeper meanings; Gallagher writes this as “affective qualities" (2015, 561) of sound, Taylor as "emotional power" (24) and Westerkamp shares that she found the use of environmental sounds "irresistible” (51).

Observing Semantics in Practice: 3 Short Analyses

The analyses of three selected works below serve as a demonstration of the effectiveness of the theoretical model proposed in this paper. As such, each analysis comprises three aspects to consistently trace a path from original sound to its arrangement inside a musical composition:

  • subtractive essense of the recorded sound’s production
  • identification of semantic information via semantic parameters;
  • exploration of varied audience interpretations.

A Collage of Semantic Cues: Annea Lockwood’s For Ruth (2021)

Annea Lockwood (b. 1939) is a New-Zealand born American composer. Her compositions are centered on natural sounds, especially the sound of water, and her musical practice is heavily based in working with natural sounds. Lockwood’s process for recording sound for her composition For Ruth is detailed in the liner notes of the recording. In other words, the written notes accompanying the music provide a listener with the means to infer semantic knowledge. This does not replace the recognition process, but offers a route to fill gaps where recognition is not happening.

The soundscape of this piece is made up of clips from her late partner Ruth Anderson’s work entitled Conversations and natural sounds from Hancock, New Hampshire and Flathead Lake, Montana, two places significant to the couple. It is also mentioned in the notes that Flathead Lake is Anderson’s resting place. The subtractive-ness of these sounds can be considered by their extraction from Lockwood’s daily life and memories into a recorded format— Lockwood is filtering down places which are extremely significant for her, and this significance is communicated through the attached notes.

In addition to the recordings of nature gathered by Lockwood, she also takes clips from Ruth Anderson’s Conversations— which itself is a collage extracting audio from their phone calls and popular tunes into a piece of music. What we can see is a cascading layer of filtering sound out from emotionally intense and, in this case, quite intimate settings.

This piece of music is saturated with semantic cues which can appropriately be analysed by considering them as space or place. Numerous identifiable sonic objects like bird calls, voices over the telephone, and water all point towards sonic spatial environments which shift throughout the piece, indicating movement. A listener can construct mental images of these spaces using the offered cues— not just that a space is outdoors, but also the listeners’ position in that space. Segments where water can be heard closely communicate where in the space the recording is.

As far as place goes, this is a situation in which the meanings behind the sonic signs is highly dependant on the semantic knowledge possessed: if a listener knows where these sounds come from (via the liner notes), the sounds would take on the meaning of place, pointing to the specific locations in which Lockwood and Anderson spent their lives together. This emotional intimacy can then be considered to be woven into the composition through the meaning of the environmental sounds. This is reinforced compositionally by the spoken word in the phone calls, where a listener could connect emotions heard through tone of voice to emotions from the locations being recorded: Flathead Lake and Hancock where the couple lived.

On the other hand, in a situation where a listener does not possess the semantic knowledge, these sounds are more spaces than places. However, this does not diminish any possibility for interpretation: listeners can still choose to correlate environmental cues, spatial cues, and linguistic cues in a semantic method just as significant as being aware of the composer’s intentions. The meanings of the sounds of birds, natural spaces, running water can still create a tapestry upon which the snippets of telephone call (which is more a place than a language— the audio quality harkens to communication via technology which is a place with plenty specificity) does sit and present their linguistic meanings.

Lockwood’s field recordings here present a sonic collage which is not dependent upon the liner notes for any sort of “true” understanding. The supplication of the piece’s background is simply another layer of depth to potential audience interpretation to a potential audience interpretation.

Language as a Primary Semantic Parameter: Annika Socolofsky’s so much more (2022)

Scottish-born composer Annika Socolofky’s (b. 1990) work so much more weaves together a performed score for reed quintet and a recorded track made up of interviews with small business owners in the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic. It is extremely saturated with linguistic semantics through its track, entirely made up of cleanly recorded voices. The nature of the audio allows it to be interpreted similarly to either radio show, a documentary film, or as lyrics/poetry in the ways sentences are stitched together. One thing is clear: the sound is not just the interview itself, but the origin of the content being discussed.

The key criteria for recognition here is an understanding of the English language, but there is a secondary layer in which lies references to elements of place — both naming places like Detroit, and portraying implicitly specific communities like small business and local neighborhoods. Language is the primary driver of communication here, but beyond the semantics of each word, there are sonic elements which harken to place. A prominent one is the element of accent. As Pavitra Sundar (2023) notes, an accent has an effect on the sonic character of spoken language while the meaning may stay the same. Throughout Socolofky’s composition, numerous places are represented — a farmer in Kansas, a wigmaker in North Carolina with an Asian accent, an LGTBQ+ wellness center owner in Colorado, and four business owners from Detroit, Michigan. The diversity of speakers in this recording gives the semantic nature of the sound more depth and nuance.

Other noteworthy aspects in the piece are the featured individual stories. Particularly, the fifth movement begins with the story of a wigmaker receiving a letter from a dying young girl with leukemia wanting to thank the woman making her wigs. While this is extremely touching, the question as to what semantic parameters can be discerned is almost overloaded. Taking words of this nature and simply describing it as linguistic semantics doesn’t truly capture the depth of its psychological effect (or what Gallagher (2015) might term its “affective quality”). As I conveyed above, the three semantic parameters I focus on in this article do not comprise a comprehensive list, and complex sonic cues like this one are not fulfilled by single aspects of semantic decoding, offering an opportunity for further exploration, identification, and categorization. This complexity is aligned with Einbond’s (2013) description of sources as “totalities” (58).

Semantics of Fear in Soundscape: Henrik Hellstenius’s Places of Sounds and Words (2014)

Norwegian composer Henrik Hellstenius’ (b. 1963) piece contrasts with the two previously discussed works as, at times, his field recordings are woven into the composition texture in a much more subtle way. This makes moments where the field recordings are front and center that much more effective throughout the work’s seven movements. I would like to single out the fourth movement which uses audio from a call to emergency services with what sounds like shouting in the background. This is not the only moment in the piece evoking fear, but it is the most pronounced of the field recordings in depicting clear semantic cues.

The subtractive nature of these sounds are not as clear as in the previous compositions — there are no liner notes or explicit linguistic information to dissect — so the subtractiveness of the sounds is likely to lie more in the recording process and the technique of the sound collage. The 911 call more explicitly references a larger source in the social object, i.e., emergency services and perhaps domestic violence, but this is dependent on listeners interpreting the sound as such. Consistently throughout the piece, the sounds of crowds are used, but not active voices as much as the sound of movement: walking, footsteps, the brushing of fabric as people move through a space. The large quantity of small sonic events (a crowd made up of many individual footsteps, in this case) represents the idea of “totality” (Einbond 2013, 58) well. The neutrality of these unidentifiable individual moments forming a crowd in acoustic space is a good demonstration of the effect of recording as a subtractive medium.

In contrast to the title, this piece demonstrates the use of semantic space effectively, while obscuring semantic place. The neutrality or sense of blankness that is created from ambient crowd noise engenders a special sonic space for a listener to place their auditory experience. Over the course of the whole piece, this sense of space is manipulated not through the modulation of reverberation parameters, but by crafting new spaces from recorded sound. Another key element to its semantic manipulation is the presence of semantic information outside of the recording. The Soprano part, with text by Hellstenius and Jeanne de Salzmann, occupies another semantic space equal to anything being decoded from the recorded sounds. Where spaces are heard and then fade away, the soprano’s function seems to fill the space before the next spaces are constructed.

This opportunity for listeners to hear a sparse environment that is carefully constructed to undulate between recognizable as real and abstractly fake, means that interpretations, focused on the various elements of the piece, are fair game. Listeners can be captivated by the fourth movement's clarity of fear, the liminal spaces which frame the compositions by movements one and seven, or the soprano's text throughout, thus offering a route in which the field recording is less significant. While the spaces often heard are less akin to the acoustic spaces scientifically detailed in textbooks, they are still physical spaces which lack specificity — in this case, supporting the aesthetics of the music by obscuring recognition instead of clarifying it.

Conclusion

The model constructed from source to listener, and the ways in which listeners travel back up that model to interpret music via its sources, is theory at best. However, the applications of this proposed theory illuminate a manner by which composers dissect the sounds they are using to construct tapes. While field recording music and electroacoustic music are indeed separate practices with their own histories, in this case, the two intersect in their ability to convey semantic information to the listener. Lockwood’s field recording music, heard on tape alone, is matched with Hellstenius’ piece which is intended for live performance because they both use sounds to communicate similar types of information in service of their art.

The diverse scholarship which the proposed model has been constructed evidences its interdisciplinary. I believe this to benefit, first and foremost, the composers, who may want to take into consideration the power of recognizing objects in sounds and take development of their music into other fields. A piece which uses language heavily, like Socolofsky’s, can be composed using processes that screenwriters use to construct dialogue, or radio editors to construct their documentary segments. A piece like Lockwood’s can take into account how something like Feld’s “acoustemology” directly impacts the way listeners interpret culturally relevant sounds. Instead of interpolating the conclusions of other fields as it may be applied to music, they may be directly applied onto the recognition and interpretation process which stands between the listener and the source of a sound.

When a listener recognizes sound, the recording functions as a sign representing the source of that sound. This is no different from the semantic functions written symbols, but in music it communicates emotion, mood, space, and place alongside language. Using this process inside of musical composition is a powerful and effective tool.
